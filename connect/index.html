Your table export app runs on Connect. What that means.

Atlassian is retiring the Connect framework. If your Confluence admin has started seeing warnings about installed apps, this page explains what is actually happening, how to check your own site, and what a move would involve.

We make a table export app ourselves, so read this with that in mind. Everything below is taken from Atlassian's own announcements and linked so you can check it.

What Atlassian has announced

  • Since 17 September 2025: new Cloud apps can no longer be listed on the Marketplace if they contain Connect modules.
  • Since 31 March 2026: vendors can no longer push updates to Connect apps. An app that has a bug today cannot be fixed on Connect.
  • From December 2026: Connect reaches end of support. It stops receiving security patches, and Atlassian describes it as "use at your own risk". Individual features may stop working over time as Connect falls behind the platform.

There is no shutdown date. Your app does not stop working on a particular morning. What ends is maintenance: no security updates, and no way for the vendor to fix anything, even if they want to.

Sources: Announcing Connect End of Support: Timeline and Next Steps and Connect End of Support: what it means for private and custom apps.

How to check your own site

You need Confluence admin rights. This takes about a minute and changes nothing.

  1. Go to admin.atlassian.com and select your site.
  2. Select Apps, then Connected apps.
  3. Look for apps flagged as built on Connect. Atlassian has started showing these warnings inside Confluence and in the admin area.

If an app appears there and its last update on the Marketplace is older than March 2026, it will not receive another update, because vendors can no longer publish one.

Two questions worth asking your vendor

Before you move anything, it is fair to ask. Some vendors are building Forge replacements and will tell you so.

  1. Is there a Forge version of this app, and when will it be available?
  2. If not, what do you recommend to your customers?

What moving actually costs, for an export tool

This is the part that surprises people. Moving between Confluence apps is usually painful because data and configuration live inside the app. For a table export tool, that is not the case.

  • There is no data to migrate. An export tool reads tables that already exist on your pages and turns them into a file. Your tables are Confluence content and stay exactly where they are.
  • There is nothing to reconfigure. No macros are placed in your pages, so nothing breaks when the app is removed.
  • Files you already downloaded are unaffected. They are ordinary Excel and CSV files on your own devices.

In practice a move is: uninstall the old app, install a new one, export again. If you try a different app first and prefer the old one, nothing is lost either way.

This applies to any table export app, not only ours. It is a property of the category.

What to compare when you look at alternatives

Rather than a feature table, here are the five things that actually differ between table export tools, and which we found matter most when reading public reviews of apps in this area:

  1. Merged cells. Tables with merged cells are where exports usually go wrong. Test with your most complicated table, not a simple one.
  2. Numbers. Does 1.234,56 arrive in Excel as a number you can add up, or as text? Does it depend on your language?
  3. Where the data goes. Does the app send your page content to the vendor's servers, or is the file built in your browser? Check the app's privacy page and whether it carries the Runs on Atlassian badge.
  4. Large pages. What happens on a page with thousands of rows? A clear limit with a message is better than a frozen browser.
  5. Macro tables. Tables produced by other apps (Jira issue tables, table filter macros) are often not part of the page content an export tool can read. A good app tells you when it skipped something.

Where we stand

Our app is built on Forge, so it is not affected by the Connect timeline. It reads the page in your browser with your own permissions and builds the file there. No page content reaches us, and we store nothing.

What it does not do: PDF, JSON or HTML output, editing tables, filtering or charts. If you need those, apps like the ones you are already using may suit you better, and that is a perfectly reasonable outcome of reading this page.

The documentation lists the limits and known gaps in full, including what happens with macro tables and very large pages.
